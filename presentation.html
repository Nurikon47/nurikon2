The “Listening State” and Student Perceptions of Government Responsiveness

How undergraduate students in Astana interpret public-feedback channels and their potential influence on government decisions

Presentation #1 · Interpretivist Research Design

Nursultan Kuandyk

The empirical puzzle

Responsiveness versus influence

Top-down

Policy Promise

The “Listening State” presents the government as responsive to public concerns and citizen feedback.

Bottom-up

Uncertainty

Citizens may submit complaints, comments, or suggestions without knowing whether their feedback affects government decisions.

Core insight

Why might public-feedback channels exist while young citizens still doubt that their voices create meaningful influence?

Research question

What I am asking

“How do undergraduate students in Astana interpret the idea of a “Listening State,” and what criteria do they use to assess whether public-feedback channels influence government decisions in Kazakhstan in 2026?”

Key focus areas
Population

Undergraduate students in Astana.

Phenomenon

Interpretations of government responsiveness and citizen influence.

Context

Public-feedback and participation channels in Kazakhstan.

Time

Students’ perspectives in 2026.

Epistemological positioning

Why interpretivism fits

Interpretivism

Why this approach fits

1

Meaning over measurement

The study examines how students interpret “being heard,” not only how many feedback submissions exist.

2

Multiple meanings

Responsiveness may mean an official reply to one student and visible policy change to another.

3

Agency and context

Students’ interpretations may be shaped by trust, experience, political interest, and social context.

Contribution

Three contributions

1

From institutions to citizens

Shifts attention from the design of feedback institutions to how citizens interpret them.

2

Feedback and political efficacy

Examines whether students believe they can influence government decisions.

3

Youth perspective

Provides a situated account of how undergraduate students in Astana understand political participation in Kazakhstan.

Analytical framework

Sensitising concepts

Responsiveness

What counts as a meaningful government response?

Voice

Do students feel able to express their opinions?

Trust

Do students believe institutions use feedback seriously?

Political efficacy

Do students believe citizens can influence decisions?

Symbolic vs substantive participation

Is participation only formal, or does it produce meaningful engagement or change?

What I expect, and could be wrong about

Interpretivist expectations

1

Formal responsiveness may differ from meaningful responsiveness

An official reply may not be enough for students to feel heard.

2

Trust may shape participation

Students who distrust institutions may avoid formal feedback channels.

3

Visible outcomes may define influence

Students may look for policy changes, explanations, public acknowledgment, or evidence that feedback was considered.

Positivist hypothesis

A testable prediction about relationships between measured variables.

Interpretivist expectation

A transparent starting point, open to being confirmed, challenged or revised.
